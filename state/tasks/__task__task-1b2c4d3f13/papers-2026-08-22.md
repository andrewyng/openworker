## 1. Quantum computing

**Proper Learning of Shallow All-to-All Quantum Circuits**
Steven Kordonowy, Jacob Watkins — [arXiv:2608.20162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20162)
A clean sharp boundary for the ensemble behind several recent quantum-cryptographic proposals (circuit-learning hardness): given query access and known layout, a meta-algorithm of iterative front/back local gate inversions has a *discrete* learnability cliff at depth *d\* ≈ log₂ n + log₂ log₂ n*, shown via lightcone analysis. Two things make it land differently from the two complexity results I've been tracking: it is a *learning-theoretic* boundary rather than a query-complexity one, and the paper is explicit that the setting differs from the cryptographic ones it threatens — that honesty is what makes it a useful input to the ongoing "circuit-learning as a primitive" debate rather than a rebuttal. The lightcone-growth-as-learnability mechanism is a transferable tool for any result where depth-structure is the underlying quantity.

**Logarithmic depth compression of Heisenberg Hamiltonian simulation by fan-out parallelization, with built-in error detection**
Artemiy Burov, Clément Javerzac — [arXiv:2608.20250](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20250)
The right shape of result for where NISQ compilation is heading: instead of optimizing gate count, they trade depth for width using *repetition-code registers of the physical qubits* sized by interaction degree, so all same-type Pauli interactions of a hub can run in parallel after one log-depth CNOT fan-out, and the redundancy gives post-selection error detection for free. On the 13-spin TMS benchmark and a 13-spin star, the deeper sequential compile is beaten on two-qubit depth by 2–2.5× (heavy-hex / all-to-all) and volume by 1.7×. The honest caveat in the abstract is the part I care about: the win only exists *after* post-selection AND after error rates improve by 1–1.5 orders of magnitude from current calibration — this is one of the few papers that is explicit about the hardware condition under which its own result activates.

**Large Scale Entanglement Structure Detection in 100-Qubit Systems via Local Joint Measurements**
Rui Li, Yuhang Wang, Chunxiao Du, Shikun Zhang, Zheng Qin, Wenxiu Li, Hao Zhang, Zhisong Xiao — [arXiv:2608.20170](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20170)
A scaling claim that's unusually concrete: a single-basis local-Pauli protocol whose boundary-matching condition *p₁ = p_R* lets them read an entire chain in one configuration, so the measurement cost is size-independent. >95% accuracy across GHZ / W / cluster-type structure classification on up to 100 qubits in simulation, then validated on a superconducting processor up to 13 qubits before noise/depth kills it. The 13-qubit number is the real result and the honest one — that's where the hardware boundary of the method actually sits. Worth tracking as the natural tool for QEC code verification at intermediate sizes (qLDPC verification is going to need exactly this kind of structure-level readout).

**Learning Potts Models and Z₃ Toric Codes: Higher and Ordinary Nishimori Criticality**
Rushikesh A. Patil, Malte Pütz, Rohit Mukherjee, Guo-Yi Zhu, Simon Trebst, Andreas W. W. Ludwig — [arXiv:2608.20268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20268)
The cleanest bridge I've seen this month between statistical-inference learning theory and QEC code memory. Framing a monitored-deformed Zq toric code as a learning problem in the Potts model, they find a *tricritical* "higher Nishimori" information-critical point at finite inference strength that separates stable quantum-mem, classical-mem, and no-mem phases — using the Casimir effective central charge as a monotone order parameter along measurement-induced RG flows. That's a new organizing principle for which measurement protocols give you which kind of quantum memory at which temperature, and the Elitzur-theorem argument for the stability of the ordinary critical point is the kind of structural result QEC people don't get enough of.

## 2. Machine learning

**Which Eviction Policy Should an LLM Cache Use? A Systematic Study Across Workloads, Capacities, and Encoders**
Yash Kulkarni, Shubham Harkare, Arvind Suresh Yogesh Babu — [arXiv:2608.20280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20280)
The most important number here is the *negative* one: across 18 configurations of policy × workload × capacity × encoder, no evaluated eviction policy improves on LFU by more than 0.041 points. More consequential is the audit that follows — at MiniLM's median nearest-neighbour threshold, only **2.1–3.9% of sampled LMSYS/QQP hits are actually answer-substitutable**, dropping the reported 51–60% raw hit rate to a *quality-adjusted* 1.1–2.2%. The practical implication is a re-ordering: the interesting question about any semantic cache deployment is *answer validity*, not eviction policy, and the second only becomes visible once you measure the first. The proof that a new insert cannot have a resident neighbour within the hit radius (the "missing redundancy signal") is a clean geometric explanation for why clever eviction rules can't do better in this setting.
