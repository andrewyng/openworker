## 1. Quantum computing

**ON-FOCUS — Exponential-in-Nc² cost reduction of product-formula-based quantum simulations of QCD**
Zohreh Davoudi, Jesse R. Stryker — [arXiv:2608.21258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21258)
A ~10¹⁴× cut in the per-Trotter-step T-gate estimate for SU(Nc) lattice gauge theory, which they attribute to a fixable inefficiency in the Kan–Nam (2021) bound rather than a new asymptotic trick: exponentiated-Hamiltonian decomposition — the unglamorous step every product-formula route needs — can be done far more cheaply than the standard analysis assumes. The reduction is *parameter-independent* (independent of cutoff and system size), which is the strong version of the claim. Why it matters here: it is the cleanest example this month of NISQ-era cost analyses being *conservative by accident*, and the same audit — "where in the chain is each step overcounting?" — is exactly the discipline the OpenEvolve scoring tiered-cost contract needs when item 3 (PySCF DFT) and item 4 (CHGNet) land at their claimed costs. A result about cost *estimates* being wrong, not cost *algorithms* improving.

**ADJACENT (1 of 3) — Neural quantum states in condensed matter: advances, best practices, and prospects**
Jonas B. Rigo, Björn J. Wurst, Rajah Nutakki, Markus Schmitt, Dante Kennes — [arXiv:2608.21291](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21291)
A perspective from the group that runs these machines (Kennes, et al.), not a new result — but the part worth a line is the *named list of remaining failures*: learning non-trivial sign/phase structure, controlling variational bias, enforcing physical symmetries, scaling optimization to large networks, and stabilizing real-time evolution. That is now the field's own checklist of where neural quantum states break, which is the prior to apply before treating "neural state + MC sampling" as a drop-in scoring tier. Keep it next to the Davoudi–Stryker cost result as the two poles of the same question — *how much can you actually trust a learned surrogate of a quantum system*.

## 2. Machine learning

**ON-FOCUS — Asymmetric Capacity Allocation in Self-Refinement Pipelines**
Zhuoyi Yang, Ian G. Harris, Salar Hashemitaheri, Cassie Huang, Yuangang Li, Hyunwoo Oh, Paul Dourish, Tony Givargis, Mohsen Imani, Li Zhang — [arXiv:2608.21345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21345)
The first stage-wise size-scaling study of the generate→critique→revise loop: 6 Qwen3 × 4 Gemma 3 sizes across 5 benchmarks, varying *which* stage gets the small model. The result is not "everyone small" or "everyone big" — it is *position-dependent*: the generator and refiner reward size, the critic barely does (a small critic beats no critic, but adding critic size buys little), and an **undersized refiner actively harms** output. That asymmetry is the load-bearing detail: it says the refinement stage — the "improve your own output" step — is the one that cannot be delegated to a cheap model, which is the inverse of what a naive local-27B fleet would assume and a direct cost/quality input for how the OpenWorker stack should split judgment vs. execution capacity. It is also the small-model twin of the Faraday pattern (flagged 2026-08-23: small judgment model + big execution tool) — the two papers together say the *allocation*, not the total, is the variable.

## 3. Recursive self-improvement

**ON-FOCUS — Designing a Robust LLM-Based Evaluation System for Agentic AI in Drug Discovery Through Human Alignment**
Emma Granqvist, Rocío Mercado, Samuel Genheden (AstraZeneca) — [arXiv:2608.21057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21057)
A deployed, human-aligned judge for ChatInvent, their agentic drug-discovery assistant — the most directly ON-FOCUS item in the batch because it is the *scoring layer this record keeps flagging as the missing piece*, built by a group that ships it. The contributions worth taking: (a) four output-quality dimensions (Completeness, Relevancy, Structural Clarity, Scope Adherence) **plus deterministic tool-call-correctness checks** — separating what a judge can and cannot reliably score; (b) they benchmark four candidate judges (Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5, Llama 3.1 70B) against 5 expert annotators rather than assuming one — and few-shot demonstrations on human-annotated examples lift alignment from 0.80 → 0.86, which is the honest magnitude of what rubric tuning can buy; (c) a negative finding that cuts against a common prior — informal user phrasings do not degrade agent output, and *pre-rewriting the question helps*, i.e., prompt hygiene upstream is worth more than judge robustness downstream. Read next to Phantom Gains (2026-08-21): that paper says "self-reported self-training gains are not trustworthy," this one says "here is the machinery that makes one trustworthy" — together they are the measurement answer the RSI field needs this month.

**ON-FOCUS — Symposium: Trust via Auditable Records for Communities of AI Scientist Agents** *[catch-up — first time in this record; posted 2026-08-20 evening, inside the 08-21 window but missed by it]*
Dexter Pratt — [arXiv:2608.19511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19511)
Not another agent — a *record-keeping layer underneath* agents: an immutable, structured history of claims, evidence citations, assumptions, and explicit evidence-scope declarations, shared by a community of agents and decoupled from the agents themselves. Its stated design assumption — "a community will use diverse AI systems in a rapidly evolving environment" — is exactly the operating condition this running record runs under, and the "auditable trail of analyses, hypotheses, data" is the provenance artifact Claude-Science-class polish argues for (FOCUS.md, entry 1; memory #6). The practical take is the *separation* — the durable record outlives and supervises the agents that write to it — which is the inverse of an agent that stores its own notes in its own context. A working implementation and prompt components are released, so it is adoptable, not just a proposal.

**ADJACENT (2 of 3) — Science Done on a Machine by a Machine: AI Agents in Computational Chemistry** *[catch-up — posted 2026-08-19]*
Pavlo O. Dral, Hassan Nawaz, Arif Ullah — [arXiv:2608.18508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18508)
A Perspective from Dral (co-creator of the most widely used ML force-field tool), surveying ~50 agentic computational-chemistry systems as of Aug 2026 and naming the two facts that will matter most for where this field is going: the specialized-agent space is being *commoditized by generalist agents* (adding a new capability is becoming "ask the model"), and *adoption beyond each system's own developers is very limited* — a quiet warning that the number of demos is not the same as the number of users. The authors end with "we have no answer" about where established specialists should invest their effort, which is the most honest framing of the disruption question I have seen this month. Worth one read as the external prior for the OpenEvolve/quantum-chem stack decision, not as a new result.

### Cross-topic observation
This run's three ON-FOCUS items are the same question seen from three angles — *how do you know your scoring layer is right* — and they are all in the scoring/verification space FOCUS.md is currently working in: Davoudi–Stryker (cost *estimates* overcounting — audit the assumption, not just the algorithm, quantum §1), Granqvist et al. (the judge itself needs a measured human-alignment baseline before it can be trusted, RSI §1), and Pratt (the trust substrate should be a shared, agent-independent record rather than each agent's own notes, RSI §2). The one new empirical variable is the *asymmetry*: which stage of the loop must be the big model (Yang et al., ML §1) — for a local-27B fleet, the answer they give is "the refiner and the generator, not the critic," which is the most specific and the most actionable single line in this digest.
