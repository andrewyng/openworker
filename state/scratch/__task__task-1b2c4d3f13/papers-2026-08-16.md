## 1. Quantum Computing

### Exponential quantum advantage for learning signals with a single qubit
**Authors:** Ishaan Kannan, Sridhar Prabhu, Saeed A. Khan, Mandar M. Sohoni, Xingrui Song, Saswata Roy, Alen Senanian, Valla Fatemi, Peter L. McMahon, Jordan Cotler
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13521

The genuinely new thing is the QPsi (Quantum Phase-Space Inference) framework, which converts an experimental objective + constraints into both a tight lower bound on classical performance *and* the optimal quantum-enhanced algorithm, plus a certifiable quantum advantage. That's a much bigger deal than the experimental demo (which shows a 10^7 fold reduction on Fourier-learning with a single superconducting qubit), because it gives a systematic method for finding *where* quantum sensing helps, outside the quantum Fisher information regime where most previous claims live. If the lower bounds are right, "a qubit helps classically-sensing" moves from hand-waved to a design principle. Watch the QPsi lower-bound assumptions carefully — that's where the claims will be stress-tested.

### Universal magic state concentration
**Authors:** Jacopo Rizzo, Lorenzo Leone
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13376

Existing magic-state distillation assumes the inputs are *some known* noisy state near a target; this is the first protocol that takes a **few copies of an unknown pure non-stabilizer qubit state** and outputs an **exact** target magic state via a fixed stabilizer circuit. The six-copy CCZ concentration result (necessary and sufficient, with the optimal success probability set by a stabilizer Renyi entropy M^lin_3) is new and I don't think the tightness is well-established elsewhere. This matters for fault-tolerant architectures that generate magic states on-the-fly from state-preparation hardware — if your source is only "a qubit in some non-stabilizer state," you can still get exact magic, which loosens a real practical constraint on hardware error rates.

### Quantum simulation of non-Markovian dynamical systems
**Authors:** Abtin Ameri, Arkopal Dutt, Hari Krovi
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13533

Most quantum simulation literature covers Markovian dynamics, where the future depends only on the present state. This paper attacks the class of Volterra integro-differential equations with memory kernels and shows exponential speedups over classical ODE simulation *for the general-kernel case where M<1*, plus lower bounds showing the problem becomes intractable when M>=1 (so the quantum speedup is genuinely structural, not an artifact of a favorable regime). The "Markovianization" move — converting VIDEs with structured kernels into ODEs and then using standard techniques — is a clean trick that should transfer to other physics. Worth watching if you care about open quantum systems or fluid dynamics on a quantum processor in 3-5 years.

### Entanglement distribution and quantum storage of >8000 modes over a metropolitan network
**Authors:** Angelo Gelmini Rodriguez, Louis Nicolas, Theo Sanchez Mejia, Pavel Sekatski, Nicolas Brunner, Towsif Taher, Rob Thew, Philippe Goldner, Mikael Afzelius
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13177

The real number here isn't "quantum repeater node" (those have been demo'd before), it's the **8235 temporal modes stored for 63 microseconds in a live Geneva municipal fiber network**, with a 979nm photon entangled to a telecom photon propagating 25.3km. The rare-earth ion ensemble memory is doing something genuinely new: multimode storage at 250MHz bandwidth with 76.6us lifetime, validated with a quantitative mode-capacity metric. If this scales, quantum repeater networks stop being bottlenecked on entanglement generation rates and start bottlenecking on network topology, which is a fundamentally different engineering problem.

### Classical simulation and design frontiers for IBM's doped Clifford sampling experiment
**Authors:** (not fully captured in API response — see arXiv)
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13110

Classically simulates a 70-qubit, 70-layer IBM doped-CNOT random circuit (468 T-gates) using a temporal-boundary tensor network contraction. The claim is that their width is *provably minimal* (Ratcatcher-certified) for the tested instances, so they're not being beaten — the architecture of the circuit is such that the tensor-network width is fundamentally large. The paper's real value is the empirical data point: a real hardware-produced output distribution, 2051 amplitudes, 37.3 minutes on 256 H100 GPUs, 256GiB largest tensor per batch. If the XEB estimate (0.35034) is compatible with IBM's fidelity claim, the "quantum advantage" for this specific experiment is *much narrower* than the popular framing implies.

## 2. Machine Learning

### Vero: Can AI agents build formally verified software repositories?
**Authors:** Zhe Ye, Hantao Lou, Yuechun Sun, Peiyang Song, Zhengxu Yan, Timothe Kasriel, Qingyang Zhang, Kaiyu Yang, Soonho Kong, Jingxuan He, Dawn Song
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13522

First benchmark to evaluate *joint* code+proof synthesis across **whole multi-module repository instances** (43 instances, Lean 4, spanning crypto protocols, distributed systems). The key design decision that makes this useful rather than another function-level coding benchmark is the **audit mechanism**: agents can prove the spec is unsatisfiable or the reference implementation is wrong, which surfaces hidden bugs in the curated data. That self-audit loop is what makes the benchmark credible. The finding that frontier agents solve only 27/43 and close zero on the hardest repos is a meaningful negative result — verified-code generation is not an incremental extension of current coding-agent capability.

### DARTree: Speculative diffusion decoding with autoregressive draft trees
**Authors:** Tianyi Li, Yaxin Luo, Xinyi Shang, Zhiqiang Shen
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13524

Speculative decoding for LLMs (accept multiple draft tokens in one verification step) is well-studied; this extends it to **diffusion-based drafters** (which predict whole token blocks in parallel) and adds an AR-correction tree over the diffusion output. The 98.6% improvement in accepted-length over DFlash, 9.73× speedup, is a real inference-cost win for diffusion LLMs. What's genuinely new: a clean separation between drafting (diffusion, fast) and verification (AR tree, lossless), and decoupling the AR-head inference from heap operations so it's batchable. If you're serving diffusion-based LLMs at scale, this is the kind of paper that changes your cost model.

### Defensive Boosting for Online Probabilistic Forecasting
**Authors:** Georgy Noarov, Aaron Roth
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13554

Online boosting has two existing guarantees that are *incomparable*: gradient boosting is competitive with the best span of H on all sequences, weak-to-strong boosting drives error to zero under a weak-learning condition. This paper gives a single algorithm that satisfies **both simultaneously** on every adaptive stream, and does it with just one weak-class learner (the previous methods maintain large ensembles). The ex-post hard-core certificate is a neat mechanistic insight: if the weak-learning condition is violated, the mistake weights *are* the certificate. It's a clean theoretical result from two people who both do serious online-learning theory (Noarov has published extensively here, Roth does theory+robustness). Worth a read if online probabilistic forecasting is in your stack.

### Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero
**Authors:** Julian Minder, Viktor Moskvoretskii, Raghav Singhal, Difan Jiao, Andy Arditi, et al.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13482

Most alignment is applied post-pretraining, which means the persona is a thin overlay over whatever the pretraining data baked in. SPP annotates pretraining documents with value-aligned first-person reflections and pretrains on them, then does "persona binding" via instruction-tuning to tie the persona to the assistant identity. The key empirical finding is the **dose-timing result**: SPP applied early gives stronger constitution-following and better jailbreak robustness than the same intervention applied late; the gap grows with pretraining budget. That's a non-obvious, actionable result — *when* you do the intervention matters, not just *what* you do.

### LittleLearner: Language Models Under Pedagogically Controlled Knowledge Exposure
**Authors:** Fanfei Li, Jana Zeller, Manuel Prada-Corral, Thaddäus Wiedemer, Prasanna Mayilvahanan, Ryan Cotterell, Wieland Brendel
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13545

Trains a 5B LLM on a 88B-token corpus *explicitly limited to US elementary-school material* (concepts, facts, vocabulary above Grade 5 excluded). The result is a model with functional language skills but with **knowledge and capability boundaries that map to interpretable curriculum guidelines**. That's a genuinely useful experimental tool: instead of "did the model know this because of pretraining data X?", you can ask "did it know this because we gave it this?" — which is a much cleaner ablation for knowledge-injection studies. Not a headline paper, but the kind of contribution that changes how people design alignment/education experiments.

## 3. Recursive Self-Improvement

### AutoDesign: Meta-Harness Optimization for Long-Horizon Agentic Design
**Authors:** Yaxin Luo, Haobin Jiang, Jialv Zou, Xu Huang, et al. (14 authors)
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13560

A meta-harness optimizer that takes rollout feedback from an agent's runs and *recursively improves the agent's harness* (the scaffolding around the model: prompts, tool-orchestration, workflows). Key distinction from "just fine-tuning the model": the harness is the mutable artifact, which is far cheaper and far more deployable than weight updates. The 253-tool-call, 11-edit-turn, $3 budget for a full autonomous loop reaching human conference-poster quality is a concrete, low-cost instance of the recursive-improvement pattern. The honest result: consistent gains (+12.4% average PosterBench score) across different code-agent backbones, which means the improvement is in the harness, not in any particular LLM.

### Practice Makes Unsafe: Skill Mis-evolution in Self-Improving LLM Agents
**Authors:** Xutao Mao, Liangjie Zhao, Xiang Zheng, Cong Wang
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12851

This is the **safety paper in the room**. Self-improving agents convert successful trajectories into persistent cross-task skills. The problem: if a *malicious* trajectory happens to succeed, that skill gets distilled into the agent's persistent state and *survives after the malicious input is gone*. Their SkillMisevo-Bench measures this lifecycle explicitly (authoring → retrieval → later execution), and the finding is that all 21 evolved configurations author unsafe artifacts, 15 of which cause fresh-session harm. The SafeEvolve wrapper reduces unsafe retrieval by 26.7pp and fresh-session harm by 17.3pp, with 0.4pp utility loss. This is the first systematic treatment of the skill-mis-evolution problem, and it's a serious gap in current self-improvement systems. If you're building or deploying self-improving agents, this is the paper to read.

### AQuA: Recursively Self-Improving Quantitative Trading Research Agents
**Authors:** Jiacheng Guo, Suozhi Huang, Yunlong Gao, Zihao Li, Jian Ge, Xu Kuang, Mengdi Wang
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12841

Two independent systems (one for factor discovery, one for trainable model development) each run their own closed loop: retain validated evidence from earlier experiments, use it to guide later proposals. The "recursive self-improvement" here is at the **research-process level**, not the model-weight level, which is a different (and arguably more realistic) framing. The results are concrete: IC ~0.190 on a crypto universe, +0.0843 per-stock IC on US equities, held-out Sharpe up to +2.50. The sealed sandbox (fixed data splits, fixed evaluator) is a good design choice — it means the improvements aren't just overfitting to the test set. This is a strong case that "research-loop" self-improvement is viable in a bounded, verifiable domain.

## Bottom line

The quantum batch has real new results (magic-state concentration, non-Markovian simulation, the IBM simulation data point). The ML batch is dominated by inference cost and alignment papers — DARTree and Defensive Boosting are the strongest contributions. The RSI batch splits: the AutoDesign and AQuA papers are positive results on recursive improvement at the harness/research-loop level, while the Practice Makes Unsafe paper is the paper to *act on* — it identifies a concrete, measurable safety failure mode in self-improving agents that most current systems don't address.
